An open view of Ghana's land, water and forests

Tinga Lens turns free satellite data into simple maps for every district in Ghana, and keeps them up to date automatically.

Why it exists

Satellites record rainfall, soil moisture, vegetation, fires and forest change over Ghana every day, and most of that data is free. Very little of it reaches the people who could use it, because it comes as large technical files. Tinga Lens does the processing and shows the result district by district, so that a farmer group, a district officer, a journalist or an insurer can see what is happening without specialist software.

"Tinga" means land in Gurune, a language of Ghana's Upper East Region.

How it works

Every Monday a program downloads the newest data from each source, averages it over each of Ghana's 260 districts, and compares it with earlier years. The map then shows whether a district is above, near or below what is normal for that time of year. Each layer explains its own method and limits underneath the map, and the full code is public.

The maps are guides, not official declarations. They are estimates from satellites and models, and they are still being checked against what people see on the ground.

Who runs it
